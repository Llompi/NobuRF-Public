NobuRF and openEMS compared

Both are free, open-source FDTD solvers. openEMS is installed on your computer, scripted from Python, MATLAB or Octave, and runs on the CPU. NobuRF runs in a browser tab on your GPU, and I check it against openEMS. On the same patch antenna, meshed the same way, the two land within 0.4 % of each other on resonance.

If your design is copper on a board and you'd rather not install anything, start with NobuRF. If it is mostly a 3D body, such as a horn or a cavity filter, use openEMS for now.

How close the results are

I ran one patch antenna in both codes: a 16 × 12.4 mm patch on 1.5 mm FR-4 (εr 4.4, no loss), fed by a microstrip line through a 50 Ω lumped port, with zero-thickness copper. Both used a graded mesh with 0.25 mm cells at the copper. openEMS v0.37.0 placed its mesh lines a third of a cell inside each copper edge, as its authors advise for thin metal.

Across 4.8 to 6.2 GHz the two impedance curves differ by 8.1 Ω on average for the edge-fed patch and 9.8 Ω for the inset patch, so the close match at the dip is the best point, not the whole band.

Both are FDTD codes, so this checks that NobuRF implements the method correctly. It does not check the method against a real board. For that, the validation report compares NobuRF with published measurements: the patches from Chang et al. (1986) land within 4 % of their measured resonance on substrates up to 0.11 wavelengths thick, and further off on thicker ones. A line-fed patch from Sheen et al. (1990) lands 0.3 % low. I haven't measured a board of my own on a VNA yet.

The connector's resonance is left out of the table: those openEMS runs put mesh lines on the copper edges, which reads about 1 % low, so they need a rerun before the frequencies can be compared. Curved solids, such as a sphere or a cylinder turned against the grid, are checked against exact cavity modes (within 0.07 to 0.6 %), not against openEMS yet.

Where they disagree

The demo in the app runs on a uniform 0.5 mm grid to keep it fast. On that grid NobuRF reads 0.4 to 1.3 % high against openEMS on its graded 0.25 mm mesh. The main cause is the substrate: 0.5 mm cells put only three cells across 1.5 mm, where the graded runs use six.

On a uniform 0.25 mm grid the inset patch's dip read −36.8 dB in NobuRF against −15.6 dB in openEMS, because the notch edges snapped to the grid. The graded mesh in the table puts lines on the notch edges, and the dips agree: −22.1 dB against −20.4 dB. For an inset feed, use a graded mesh.

The port model matters more than the mesh. In openEMS alone, changing the one-edge port for a port across the full feed width moves the edge-fed resonance from 5.27 to 5.41 GHz, 2.7 %. NobuRF's default is the same one-edge port openEMS uses, and the feed-wide port is an option. Any S11 comparison between two solvers has to say which port it used.

Every number here, with the scripts that produced it and the openEMS script, is in the validation report. NobuRF exports Touchstone files, so you can put its S-parameters next to an openEMS run of your own board.
